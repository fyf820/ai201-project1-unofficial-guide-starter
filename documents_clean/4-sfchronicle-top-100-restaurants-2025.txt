By MacKenzie Chung Fegan and Cesar Hernandez | Updated March 11, 2026 12:22 p.m.
As someone who, at a former job, tested a lot of kitchen appliances, I can assure you that determining the best restaurants in the Bay Area is a lot more nuanced than compiling a list of the best blenders. More art, less science, more subjectivity, fewer smoothies. Before we dive into our list of the Top 100 restaurants, my colleague Cesar Hernandez and I want to take a beat to think aloud — what even is a “top restaurant”?

Top 100 updated for 2026: Our critics rank the best restaurants in the San Francisco Bay Area
The Chronicle has been selecting cohorts of 100 since 1996, but the 2025 installment is the first since the pandemic upended the restaurant industry as we know it. It’s been six years since we’ve had a Top 100 class, and its return is a celebration of the resilience of our community, of the people behind the food we love. Scrolling through the list feels like flipping through a family photo album, a scrapbook from a year of dining out.

Each year the list has reflected the personal tastes, predilections and biases of the critic or critics behind it. This year is no different. We are but two humans, albeit two humans who eat hundreds of restaurant meals a year, with strong opinions and stronger stomachs. Sometimes we agree with the Michelin Guide or our predecessors Michael Bauer and Soleil Ho, and sometimes we don’t. Usually we agree with each other, and sometimes Cesar is wrong. We stand by this collaborative list, and we are open about our methodology.

To us, a top restaurant might be a multicourse fine dining bonanza from an internationally renowned chef or a bakery serving astonishing tortas trussed in plastic wrap to a line of construction workers. What they have in common is a commitment to doing whatever it is they do with excellence. When assembling this final list, we took into account diversity (there goes my federal grant money) of region, cuisine and price point, keeping in mind our North Star: We want this list to be useful to you, our readers, not a mere artifact or exercise. There are other lists that will tell you which restaurants are the fanciest or the buzziest, but is that really how we eat? As you seek guidance on where to find a breakfast sandwich in Napa or a date-night spot in Oakland, we hope you’ll return to the 2025 Top 100 again and again.

— MacKenzie Chung Fegan

Those pictures don’t always capture the spirit of a restaurant — the singular, sacred and sometimes messy joy of communing over food. We decided to borrow from the high-flash energy of party photography and intimate texture of film by giving disposable cameras to staff photographers, reporters, editors and restaurants themselves to document their experiences. These images are candid and imperfect glimpses of what it means to cook, to serve and to eat — with gusto.

100.Poppy Bagels
East Bay

Genre
Bagels
Price
$
Poppy Bagels
Food pundits will squabble until the end of time about whether you can get a truly stellar New York bagel outside of the five boroughs, but with the opening of Poppy Bagels in 2023, we’d argue that the more relevant question is can you find a good California bagel in New York?

Reesa Kashuk’s cheery storefront in Oakland’s Temescal neighborhood serves all the standards — lox and whitefish, flavored schmears and plain — but the thing to get is the seasonal veg sandwich. Served open face, it’s a love letter to our region’s bounty. Thinly sliced circles of radish, avocado and microgreens are crowned with a drizzle of Kuali salsa macha for added crunch and heat. It’s a California salad on a New York bagel, and it’s a breakfast very much worth seeking out.

This self-taught baker makes some of the Bay Area’s most in-demand bagels. Her first shop opens this week
5004 Telegraph Ave., Oakland

99.Shizen
San Francisco

Genre
Vegan sushi
Price
$$
Have you had vegan sushi? You should, especially from Shizen, a master of manipulating vegetables into textural marvels. The feel of the joint is lively, loose; you won’t find any of the rigidity, or quiet, of an omakase spot.

If you can, sit at the bar and appreciate the colorful stacks of okra, mango, tomatoes and squash, then order the yuba salad, peppery arugula and tender strips of tofu skin tossed in a sweet, creamy dressing. Anything with shredded tofu is exceptional, particularly the spicy tofu tempura roll called “body and soul.” The best nigiri in the house are the custardy grilled eggplant and the tomato, where the slivered fruit is taut and drained of its liquids for a satisfying chew. By the end of the meal, you’ll walk away with a deeper understanding of sushi — as an ethos, as a methodology, as a lifestyle — which can be untethered from fish.

Shizen in San Francisco makes a stellar case for vegan sushi
370 14th St., San Francisco

98.The Rotunda
San Francisco

Genre
American
Price
$$
If the Rotunda were a person, she would be the prim and proper grandmother of the bride, surely a former debutante. Then the DJ would play Usher, and she’d drop it low on the dance floor.

Located on the fourth floor of Neiman Marcus, catty-corner from Union Square, the Rotunda serves salads and a very festive afternoon tea to ladies who lunch. But the sleeper hit of the menu is the burger, a sloppy, double-patty behemoth that is impossible to eat daintily. Like everything else at the Rotunda, it is served with a complimentary demitasse of chicken consommé, a popover with strawberry butter and a square of chocolate. How refined.

97.FOB Kitchen
East Bay

Genre
Filipino
Price
$$
Amaya Edwards / For the S.F. Chronicle
It’s hard to be in a bad mood at contemporary Filipino restaurant FOB Kitchen. Perhaps the feel-good charm comes from the tropical ambiance, with descending plant vines and aquatic blue tiles that conjure coastal images; or maybe it’s the crackle of lumpia, the svelte richness of pork adobo or the spicy, vinegary bite of its in-house bottled hot sauce.

All of these elements help eliminate tension in the mind and shoulders, and the cocktail program keeps the party going, with a standout riff on a piña colada that adds the vanilla-like flavor of pandan. FOB Kitchen is a place of celebration.

96.Mandalay
San Francisco

Genre
Burmese
Price
$$
Mandalay, the longest running Burmese restaurant in the Bay Area, has been stunning diners for more than 40 years with dishes like its brain-tickling tea leaf salad: an assortment of seeds, nuts, fried garlic and pureed fermented tea leaves, mixed tableside into a textural masterpiece.

Flaky, oil-slicked paratha accompanied by slightly sweet, creamy curry dip is richly satisfying. A similar curry acts as the base for the splendid chicken noodle soup. Mandalay still sets the standard for Burmese cuisine in the Bay Area, which has flourished locally thanks to an influx of Burmese immigrants that began in the 1980s. While there are now many trendier Burmese restaurants, what keeps people coming back to Mandalay, aside from the food, is its vibrant charm: its bright interior, the color of mustard; its Burmese art and jewelry; its kitschy string lights; and its gracious service.

4348 California St., San Francisco

You’ll find Thailand’s greatest hits on the menu at this family-run restaurant on the bottom floor of an East Oakland home, but you’re at an establishment named after the capital city of Laos. Would you order the steak at a seafood restaurant?

94.Charter Oak
North Bay

Genre
American
Price
$$$
With Charter Oak, chef Christopher Kostow, most known for running the now-closed Restaurant at Meadowood, ditches the tweezers and foams for food that’s deliberately primordial. At the center of the high-ceilinged dining room lies a roaring wood-fire hearth, where broccoli, pork jowls and steaks are all baptized by flame.

There’s even a distinct tenor to the crackle of the house-baked bread, which might be the sound of the 30-year-old sourdough starter used in each loaf. Despite its focus on seasonal produce, Charter Oak also makes an incredible burger, sporting fancy dry-aged beef, garlicky pepper relish and white American cheese. With its tree-lined courtyard and ivy-covered brick walls, visiting this St. Helena restaurant feels like stumbling across a countryside chateau.

1050 Charter Oak Ave., St. Helena

93.Alem’s Coffee
East Bay

Genre
Eritrean
Price
$
Alem’s Coffee is a community fixture and a stalwart for Eritrean and East African cooking in Oakland. Since 1999, the inviting cafe has had a familiar aura, where even new visitors feel like longtime patrons.

Alem’s is home to one of the Bay Area’s greatest breakfast dishes: shihan ful, a thick mash of fava beans topped with a glug of olive oil, diced red onions, berbere spice mix, crumbled cheese and a lemon wedge for a crucial jolt of acidity. It’s accompanied by not one but two crusty sourdough rolls — the restaurant’s carb of choice and your tool for scooping starchy ful. It’s light but full flavored, with a moderate threat of heat.

5353 Claremont Ave., Oakland

Hilda and Jesse
This may not make sense if you’re not queer, but Hilda and Jesse is a gay restaurant. The sandwich board out front says as much, advertising that it uses ingredients from “queer farmers and wine producers” and serves the “gayest pancakes ever” cooked by a “soft butch chef.”

But I’m not just talking about the owners or the staff or even the vibe. The food is gay. 90.Goodtime Bar
South Bay

Genre
Californian
Price
$$
Goodtime Bar
If you could bottle up the hunger for something new in San Jose’s dining scene, you would get Goodtime Bar. This natural wine bar has attracted palpable excitement to its downtown alley location with a simple formula for a good time: crisp, zippy wine and punchy, creative small plates.

Think bucatini flavored with sour Thai tom yum seasoning; tender, cheese-stuffed arancini; and melty tuna tostadas. Inside, the place is no larger than your friend’s living room. If it fills up, as it is wont to do, more space is available out front, which is no less enthusiastic.

89.Ming’s Tasty
East Bay

Genre
Dim sum
Price
$
Barbecue buns with crusty sugar tops, frilly fried shrimp balls, chewy but tender siu mai: all reasonably priced to feed a family of bellies. Ming’s Tasty is the people’s champ of dim sum, for its affordability, consistency and no-frills aesthetic.

The Oakland Chinatown restaurant’s favor is evidenced by regular crowds of diners that spill out onto the sidewalk. (It usually doesn’t take more than 25 minutes to get a table, and it’s worth the wait.) Once inside, Ming’s has an absorbing effect, where the energetic thrum of the place blocks out the exterior world. Perhaps that’s fortuitous, as it leaves more room to engage with the exceptional dim sum on the plate.

One of the Bay Area’s best dim sum spots is a charming mystery

San Francisco

Genre
Pizza
Price
$$
Until recently, common adjectives one might reach for to describe the Outer Richmond would be “foggy” and “sleepy.” “Cool?” Only in a bring-a-layer sense. But change has come to the Avenues, and at the center is the Laundromat, slinging bagels in the morning and Detroit-inspired pizza with crisp, cheesy edges by night.

86.Cafe Colucci
East Bay

Genre
Ethiopian
Price
$
Cafe Colucci
For more than 30 years, Cafe Colucci was a beloved institution and community gathering place in Oakland. It outgrew its former building and expanded into its current larger space a few years ago. The spacious interior is decorated with images of spice farms and warm tones, from the exposed brick walls to the clay-colored walls. Its food, meanwhile, is as great as ever.

Vegetarians and vegans will be delighted to know that meat-free dishes tend to be the standout here, a natural reflection of the cuisine. Don’t miss the mushroom tibs, where the fungi soaks up the sauce bolstered with sweltering berbere spice. Carnivores, you’re welcome here too, with excellent dishes like saucy chicken or beef kitfo: silky, raw beef doused in berbere-flavored clarified butter. Scoop all of it up with sour injera, the flatbread made of teff and your edible utensil for all dishes.

Step inside this 31-year-old Ethiopian restaurant’s new Oakland home
5849 San Pablo Ave., Oakland

85.Reem’s
San Francisco

Genre
Middle Eastern
Price
$
Reem’s had a tough start to the year. In January, its 2-year-old kiosk in the Ferry Building closed after management reclaimed the space to make way for another restaurant. While this regrettably means fewer tourists will stumble upon Reem Assil’s loving tribute to Arab bakeries, the sit-down Mission District location has always been the better way to partake in the full experience.

84.Sequoia Diner
East Bay

Genre
Diner
Price
$
This is what a diner looks like when it has realized its full potential. Sequoia Diner’s foundational dishes — an ethereal yeasted waffle, the classic American breakfast plate — are exceptional, showing the Oakland restaurant maintains a firm grasp on the comfort that makes such spots so relatable. But it also looks beyond the typical Americana fare.

The loco moco, for example, flows with beefy juices that soak into fluffy white rice from Koda Farms. Ultimately, the food is so compelling because of quality ingredients and a commitment to making virtually everything from scratch, from curing pork bellies for bacon to producing three different hot sauces.

83.Besharam
San Francisco

Genre
Indian
Price
$$
Saying that Besharam is the best vegetarian restaurant in town does a disservice to chef-owner Heena Patel, like saying that she has a good arm, for a girl. Besharam, which means “shameless” in Gujarati, is a deeply personal restaurant, and since its opening in 2018, Patel’s fans have celebrated as she has made fewer and fewer concessions.

First she stepped out of the shadow of her business partner, chef Daniel Patterson, striking out on her own. Then she dropped the dishes featuring lamb, chicken or fish to better reflect the way she’s eaten her entire life — which is to say, vegetarian. Somewhere along the way she stopped dying her hair, now a regal silver that commands attention in the open kitchen. Besharam is brazen and better than ever.

Heena Patel relaunches Besharam, now with full control
1275 Minnesota St., San Francisco

82.Jubba
South Bay

Genre
Somali
Price
$
There’s only one Somali restaurant in the Bay Area. Thankfully, it’s Jubba, a fiercely flavorful jewel of the San Jose dining scene. Located in a circular building near a train station, Jubba has the easygoing, welcoming feel of a diner.

Start off with rectangular mandasi, warm, tender doughnuts with a hint of sweetness, which could also double as dessert. But the main attraction is the suqaar, morsels of chicken or beef stir-fried with peppers, onions, herbs and a generous dusting of xawaash — an earthy, toasted seasoning blend from Somalia. The ways of eating suqaar are plentiful: You can get it wrapped in spotted flatbread, or as a plate, served over fluffy rice, sour injera or, my personal favorite, a tangle of spaghetti. All plates come with a lime wedge, for a hit of brightness, and a creamy green sauce, for a jolt of heat. — C.H.

81.Mazra
Peninsula

Genre
Middle Eastern
Price
$
Run by brothers Saif and Jordan Makableh, Mazra is the Peninsula’s premier source of Levantine-style food, by way of Jordan. The operation started in San Bruno, blossoming from the closure of the Makableh family’s grocery store; it quickly made a name for its open-flame cooking, infusing smokiness into everything from spiced chicken to juicy beef kebabs and even heads of cauliflower.

For a comprehensive glimpse of its plentiful small plates, opt for the mezza sampler, with pingpong-sized crunchy falafel, toasty baba ghanoush, and tart, creamy hummus. If you’re visiting for a quick meal, a shawarma wrap, slicked with garlicky toum, might be most suitable. The San Bruno location is still closed for renovations; however, the recently opened Redwood City outpost is family friendly with a spacious dining room surrounded by plants, where light floods in from the ceiling.

Bay Area Mediterranean favorite opens lush new location
2021 Broadway, Redwood City

80.Mustards Grill
North Bay

Genre
American
Price
$$
There are many ways to dine in the Napa Valley, but none feel quite as historic or as comforting as Mustards Grill, a convivial place where creamy corn tamales, sticky glazed Mongolian pork chops and weighty burgers live in harmony.

This menu, which is mostly Americana with occasional international flair, has made the roadside restaurant vital, influencing Wine Country’s dining scene long before Thomas Keller took over the French Laundry. But perhaps more importantly, eating at the restaurant is just plain fun. The food is not self-serious, yet the level of execution is high. At once, the place feels casual and loose, formal and celebratory — a collection of competing forces that generate something memorable. Come for an impactful lunch while wine tasting, or a blockbuster dinner on any given day.

The secret to this Bay Area restaurant’s success? It hasn’t changed in 40 years
7399 St. Helena Hwy., Napa

78.Mrs Khan Uyghur Cuisine
Peninsula

Genre
Uyghur
Price
$
Mrs Khan is the Bay Area’s leading source for Uyghur food, the cuisine of the predominantly Muslim ethnic Turkic group from the autonomous region of Xinjiang. Located in downtown Menlo Park, the spacious restaurant is awash in dark wood trim, faux flowers and long tables covered with pots of mint tea, flaky meat pastries and platters of hand-pulled noodles.

The size of noodle varies per dish: The sensational, saucy chicken korma features long, thick ropes, while the earthy, spicy stir-fried beef noodles arrive as short, bouncy strings. After just one meal at Mrs Khan, you might feel like a noodle expert.

77.Table Culture Provisions
North Bay

Genre
French
Price
$$$
Lauren Segal / For the S.F. Chronicle
A good way to die would be drowning in one of the sauces at Table Culture Provisions. Stéphane Saint Louis, who runs the kitchen together with Steven Vargas, grew up in Haiti and cooked at restaurants throughout the Bay Area, but the culinary penny didn’t drop until he enrolled at the Institut Paul Bocuse in Paris.

That classic French technique is on full display in the sauces at TCP, as Petaluma locals call it, but despite its fine dining cred, the restaurant is far from stuffy. Diners croon along to smooth jazz in the intimate dining room, and on Wednesdays and Thursdays 4-6 p.m., Saint Louis and Vargas offer à la carte options for those who might not want to commit to a full tasting menu.

76.Rintaro
San Francisco

Genre
Japanese
Price
$$
Rintaro is all about craft. Step in off the street and you’re whisked away to, if not literal Japan, as close as you’re going to get south of Market Street.

The interiors at Sylvan Mishima Brackett’s restaurant are the work of his father, who studied temple carpentry in Japan. It would be worth a trip just to ogle the joinery or attempt to inconspicuously sniff the hinoki wood bar, but that’s not to discount Brackett’s cooking: exacting izakaya-inspired fare highlighting Northern California produce and proteins.

75.Itria
San Francisco

Genre
Italian
Price
$$$
As trendy as the crudos-and-pasta pairing is, the combo is at peak performance at Itria. Whereas most crudos tend to give me deja vu, Itria writes poems out of seafood.

Offerings change often but you may encounter slips of apples that add textural contrast to oysters; preserved lemon contributing salty zest to scallops; and passion fruit pulp lending tart vibrancy to silky salmon. Pastas show an equal amount of finesse, which also tend to cycle seasonally, save for the cacio e pepe mafaldine, where the peppery classic gets enriched with eggs. Natural wine is expected here, but unconventionally, the Italian restaurant also offers sake, which makes for an effective pairing. Itria seems to take pride in being different. — C.H.

Pasta and crudo restaurants are everywhere. This buzzy S.F. spot shows us why
3266 24th St., San Francisco

74.10 Butchers
South Bay

Genre
Korean barbecue
Price
$$$
Lea Suzuki / S.F Chronicle
Watching beef marbled with a web of fat sizzle on the grill is mesmerizing fun at upscale Korean barbecue restaurant 10 Butchers. The atmosphere is something like a fancy butchery meets steakhouse, except the energy is turned to, well, 10.

Premium meats are a forte of the restaurant, ranging from shaved beef tongue to thick pork jowl and A5 wagyu cuts. The staff cooks at your table, scissor-slicing meats with surgical precision into bite-size morsels. Most important: The selection of banchan is superb, with the highlights being seafood pancakes and potato salad made with sweet purple yam. Order a set of crisp lettuces to bundle grilled meats with freshness, and add on the standout galbi mandu, thick dumplings filled with sweet wagyu short rib. You’ll leave 10 Butchers with a sense of satisfaction — and the scent of barbecue on your shirt for the rest of the night.

595 E. El Camino Real, Sunnyvale

71.Cyrus
North Bay

Genre
Fine dining
Price
$$$$
Cyrus
If you were to imagine a fine dining experience in Wine Country, you might envision white tablecloths in a charming, rustic farmhouse overlooking a vineyard. Cyrus has the vineyard, but the building is more Bond villain HQ than quaint country manor.

Designed by starchitects Olson Kundig, responsible for such projects as the renovation of Seattle’s Space Needle, the building is as elemental to the meal as the food, beverages and service. Canapés are served in the bar area overlooking the vines. Diners then progress into the kitchen, where they are invited to wander between courses served at a horseshoe-shaped communal table. The heart of the meal takes place in the dining room — no white tablecloths in sight — and a parting sweet gift is dispensed in a zany, walk-in-closet-sized room with a chocolate waterfall.

70.Kunjip
South Bay

Genre
Korean
Price
$
Kunjip is a fixture in Santa Clara’s formidable Korean food scene — and just about the most regal place to eat bone broth. The dining room walls look like marble and are lined with plush booth seating; the floors are the color of brick; and a beam of light traces the perimeter of the ceiling, making the light-wood paneling glow.

There are about a dozen items on the menu, but the main draw is milky seolleongtang, for its slippery rice noodles and nourishing beef broth made with oxtails. However, cold rice noodles over an invigorating sweet and piquant sauce is the sleeper hit. Kunjip is busiest on weekends, when the strip mall crowds with eager families hoping to get their soup on, so expect to wait up to half an hour.

1066 Kiely Blvd., Santa Clara

69.Kitchen Istanbul
San Francisco

Genre
Turkish
Price
$$
If I visit Kitchen Istanbul on a Sunday or Monday, days when many restaurants in the city are closed, I’m apt to see some familiar faces. Is that the general manager of Quince? Didn’t that person sell me a bottle of wine at Saison? This neighborhood spot in the Inner Richmond is a restaurant professional’s restaurant thanks to owner Emrah Kilicoglu’s effortless hospitality and impeccable wine list.

Together with wine director Joseph DiGrigoli, Kilicoglu focuses on small producers, often with low-intervention farming and bottling practices. In my line of work, it’s impossible to be a regular at any one place, but if I could be, I’d want to be a regular here. — M.C.F.

68.Outta Sight Pizza
San Francisco

Genre
Pizza/sandwiches
Price
$
Outta Sight Pizza is a Tenderloin community jewel with a bohemian, skater energy. Not only is the food — XO escargot with milk bread, mapo spaghetti — delicious, but the energy is infectious; this is likely the most fun restaurant in the Bay Area. Long live the kings!

You know the way some people feel about their city’s pennant-winning baseball team? That’s the way we feel about Burdell. Pride is a good word for it.

When critics from other cities come to town, we delight in watching their eyes roll back in their heads as they crunch their way through Burdell’s cheeky spin on chicken and waffles. When a New Orleans-based food writer declares the barbecue shrimp to be better than back home, our chests puff with pride.

Geoff Davis’ personal take on soul food is an ode to his grandmother, culinarily and stylistically. Drenched in amber tones, the interior has retro charm mixed with the intimacy of a grandparent’s living room — evidenced by a gallery wall of the staff’s family portraits. At Burdell, a stereo system from the ’70s makes music sound better, church pews tracing the walls create a sense of communal dining and the eye-catching lime green bar is just plain pretty.

Davis’ cooking expands the scope of soul food — a true American cuisine — by highlighting its often obscured agrarian roots and infusing it with a sense of place. In the winter, salads are composed of beets and California citrus. Collards are seasoned with berbere, threading together staples of the American South and the Horn of Africa. It might seem a little odd to be proud of a restaurant where we are merely patrons, but we couldn’t be more chuffed that Burdell is representing the Bay Area on a national stage.

About this project
At least one of the San Francisco Chronicle’s restaurant critics, MacKenzie Chung Fegan and Cesar Hernandez, visited every restaurant on the Top 100. They try to experience restaurants like a typical diner, making reservations under different names. They don’t accept free meals. The Chronicle pays for all of their research.

They wait three weeks until after a restaurant opens for review visits; their cutoff for consideration for this list was Dec. 1, 2024.

To come up with the price designations, the Chronicle estimated what two people would spend on a meal without drinks or dessert. In cases where a restaurant offers different price points — for example, Troubadour Bread & Bistro’s sandwiches at lunch versus tasting menu at dinner — the critics chose the option that they most recommend.

Each restaurant on this list was photographed on a disposable camera.
